Module 1 · The fraud landscape

What financial fraud looks like today

Impersonation & deception

The victim is persuaded to send money themselves: a fake official, a relative “in trouble”, a fake investment. Authorised push payment fraud defeats authentication completely.

Stolen or synthetic identity

Accounts or cards opened or taken over with someone else's details, or with identities assembled from real and generated fragments.

Laundering the proceeds

Money from any crime moved through chains of mule accounts until its origin is hidden: placement, layering, integration.

Insider abuse

Someone inside a bank or department uses legitimate access to move or conceal funds, or trades on confidential information.

Card & payment fraud

Card testing, card-not-present fraud, merchant collusion, UPI scams and QR-code swaps.

Claims & investment fraud

Organised insurance claims, Ponzi and “guaranteed return” schemes, fake trading platforms.

Procurement

Bids suspiciously close, suppliers formed shortly before an award, contracts split just below approval limits.

Invoicing

The same invoice paid twice, near-identical invoices from “unrelated” suppliers, amounts with no delivery record.

Payroll

Ghost employees, or several employees sharing one bank account.

Revenue & licensing

Under-declaration, undisclosed links to decision-makers, payments routed through intermediaries.

Often the best place to start: public financial records are already held by the institution, already structured, and need no outside party's data. Several of these checks can be run with tools a finance department already has.
The common point: in every one of these, money passes through a financial channel at some moment. That moment is where it can be seen, and where it can still be stopped. Detection does not require catching the criminal; it requires noticing the transaction while it is still reversible.

Why rules struggle

  • Volume: manual review can inspect a fraction of one percent
  • Mutation: rings probe controls and adapt within hours
  • Interaction: signal lives in combinations of weak features no one can enumerate
  • False positives: aggressive rules decline genuine customers, a cost missing from fraud reports

Rule-based vs learned control

Rules: each new pattern needs a new rule, written after the loss. Coverage grows with analyst effort.

Models: infer a boundary from data and generalise to variants never shown explicitly. Coverage grows with data.

Honest position: rules encode what is already known; models address what is not yet articulated. Production systems run both. Anyone promising model-only fraud control is selling something.
Module 2 · AI & ML foundations

AI, machine learning, deep learning: what is inside what

Ordinary software follows instructions a person wrote. A learning system works out the instructions itself by studying past examples. Tap each ring.

Tap a ring.

Where the features come from

Classical ML consumes features an analyst engineered: transaction velocity, distance from the previous point of sale, hour of day. Deep learning derives its own representation from raw sequences, images or text.

Why the distinction is regulatory

Engineered features can be described to a customer, an ombudsman and a supervisor. Learned representations often cannot. Institutions deploy deep models where raw accuracy dominates, and interpretable models where a decision must be justified.

Resist the framing that deep learning is always superior. On tabular fraud data it often is not.
Module 2 · AI & ML foundations

How a system learns: the new-investigator analogy

A new officer

  • Reviews hundreds of past files
  • Is told the outcome of each
  • Develops an instinct for which cases deserve attention
  • Improves as new cases close

A learning system

  • Reads millions of past transactions
  • Is told which were confirmed as fraud
  • Works out which combinations accompany fraud
  • Improves as new outcomes are recorded

The system reads far more and never tires. It has no judgement, no context and no common sense; those stay with the officer.

The same idea, stated precisely

SymbolMeaningIn fraud terms
x ∈ ℝᵈFeature vector for one observationA scored transaction, claim, login or account
y ∈ {0,1}Label1 = confirmed fraud, 0 = presumed genuine
f(x; θ)Model with parameters θThe scorer in the authorisation path
p̂ = P(y=1|x)Predicted probabilityThe fraud score, before any threshold
τDecision thresholdScore above which a payment is held or challenged
πBase rate P(y=1)Fraud prevalence, typically below 0.01
f* = argminf 𝔼(x,y)~P [ ℓ( f(x), y ) ]

We want the function with the lowest expected loss on new data from the same distribution. We can never observe that distribution, only a finite sample of it. Everything that follows is an attempt to approximate it honestly.

Practical reading: a model does not learn “fraud”. It learns the conditional distribution of a label given features, in one portfolio, during one period. Throughout, the positive class is fraud, the rare class.

Three ways a machine learns

Supervised

Labelled data; learns a mapping f: X → Y.

Fraud use: card scoring, claim classification, document verification.

Limit: recognises only what resembles its labels.

Unsupervised

No labels; learns the structure of normal and reports deviation.

Fraud use: novel typologies, laundering rings, segmentation.

Limit: an anomaly is not necessarily a crime.

Reinforcement

Learns a policy from reward and penalty.

Fraud use: adaptive allow/challenge/block strategies, review routing.

Limit: rarely deployed alone in regulated decisions.

The label caution: survivorship bias

Labelled fraud data is not ground truth; it is fraud that was detected. Everything the incumbent system missed is silently labelled genuine, so a new model inherits the blind spots of whatever preceded it. This is also why measured recall is always optimistic.

A fraud engine publishes a score, not a verdict

Classification: a category

  • Is this transaction fraudulent?
  • Which typology does this claim match?
  • Is this identity document genuine?

Metrics: precision, recall, F1, AUC-PR, log loss

Regression: a number

  • Expected loss on this exposure
  • Likely settlement value of a claim
  • Tomorrow's alert volume, for staffing

Metrics: RMSE, MAE, R², quantile loss

Who owns the threshold? Risk and compliance, not the modelling team. Moving it trades customer friction against loss, and that trade is a business judgement, not a technical one. The model is fixed at training time; the threshold is a live control.

What the optimiser is actually rewarded for

The loss function defines what “good” means during training. Choosing it is a modelling decision, not a default.

LossFormWhy it matters in fraud
Log loss−[y log p̂ + (1−y) log(1−p̂)]A proper scoring rule, so it yields calibrated probabilities. The default.
Weighted cross-entropy−[w·y log p̂ + (1−y) log(1−p̂)]Raises the price of missing fraud. The simplest imbalance correction.
Focal loss−α(1−p̂)^γ y log p̂ − …Down-weights easy negatives so learning concentrates on hard cases.
Expected costC_FN·FN + C_FP·FPEncodes the real economics when both error costs are known.

Empirical risk and the generalisation gap

R̂(f) = (1/n) Σ ℓ( f(xᵢ), yᵢ )     R(f) ≤ R̂(f) + complexity_penalty(𝓕, n)

Implication 1

A flexible enough model can drive training error to zero while learning nothing transferable. Perfect training accuracy is a warning.

Implication 2

With fraud under 1%, the effective sample size is the number of positives. Ten million rows with 800 frauds is a small dataset.

Implication 3

Budget model complexity against the positives available. This is why modest-depth boosted trees beat large networks on most fraud tables.

Bias, variance and overfitting, live

𝔼[(y − f̂(x))²] = Bias[f̂(x)]² + Var[f̂(x)] + σ²

High bias: underfitting

Too rigid for the boundary. Poor on training and validation. A single shallow tree on a portfolio with many typologies.

High variance: overfitting

Memorises sample noise. Excellent in training, poor in validation. A deep unpruned tree fitting individual merchant IDs.

Module 2 · AI & ML foundations

Capabilities, limits and four misconceptions

It can

  • Examine every transaction, not a sample
  • Operate continuously
  • Notice combinations too subtle to see by eye
  • Rank cases so the most serious come first
  • Compare a person against their own history
  • Connect accounts that appear unrelated

It cannot

  • Prove a crime occurred
  • Understand context or intention
  • Explain itself unless designed to
  • Work without good data and recorded outcomes
  • Replace an officer's judgement
  • Be trusted blindly on an irreversible decision

Flip the myth

The system produces suspicion. Only a person produces a conclusion.

Anatomy of one transaction

Raw fields

Timestamp, amount, currency, merchant ID and category, channel, entry mode, BIN, account ID.

Derived fields

Amount z-score vs the customer's own history, time since previous transaction, distance from previous location, merchant novelty.

Contextual fields

Device reputation, IP and ASN, session behaviour, shared-attribute links, negative-list standing.

The core insight: a ₹50,000 payment is unremarkable for one customer and a strong signal for another. Only a per-entity baseline separates them, and the raw record contains no baseline. Almost all predictive power is created by comparison.

Module 3 · Data & features

Devices, sessions and messy data

Why device and session matter

Account takeover leaves almost no trace in transaction data: the credentials are correct and the payment may be ordinary. The deviation is in how the session was conducted: a new device, copy-paste in the beneficiary field, form completion speed, SIM change.

Strongest single indicator: a never-seen device performing a high-value or new-beneficiary action within minutes of first use.

Privacy boundary

Keystroke dynamics, touch pressure and mouse movement are personal data. Under India's DPDP Act, treat biometric-adjacent telemetry as processing that needs notice and purpose limitation, not merely a technical integration.

Missing data is often the signal

Module 3 · Data & features

If it looks too good, it is leakage until proven otherwise

Leakage is using, in training, information that would not exist at the moment of prediction. It is the most common reason a model shines in development and fails in deployment.

TypeHow it happensWarning sign
Target leakageA feature encodes the outcome: chargeback flag, investigation statusImplausibly high AUC; one feature dominates
Temporal leakageAggregates include the event itself or later eventsPerformance collapses under a chronological split
ContaminationScaling, imputation or SMOTE fitted before the splitValidation and test diverge sharply
Group leakageSame card, device or ring on both sidesGrouped split cuts measured performance
Label maturityEvaluation period whose labels are incompleteFraud rate lower than the historical norm

Spot the leak

The highest-value pre-deployment check: score historical transactions through the production feature path and compare them with the training data row by row. Training–serving skew found here is cheap; found after go-live it is not. A feature store exists to remove this skew.

Effective sample size

Estimates for the fraud class are governed by the number of positives, not rows.

Wide intervals

Recall measured on 200 test frauds carries an interval several points wide. A one-point difference between models is noise.

Base-rate dependence

Precision depends on prevalence; recall does not. That is why a vendor's precision rarely reproduces in your portfolio.

Put money on it

Why PR beats ROC here

FPR divides by true negatives, an enormous number, so thousands of false alarms barely move the ROC curve. Precision divides by alerts, so every false alarm shows. Report AUC-PR as the headline.

Bands, not a single line

Allow below τ₁, challenge (OTP, call-back) between τ₁ and τ₂, block above τ₂. Most value sits in the middle band: one extra question rarely upsets an honest customer.

Capacity is the constraint. A model producing 2,000 alerts for a team that reviews 200 has not improved detection; it has moved the bottleneck. Compare models on precision@k, where k is what your team can actually work.

Resampling, class weights and calibration

MethodMechanismRisk
Random undersamplingDiscard majority rowsThrows away information
Random oversamplingDuplicate fraud rowsMemorises individual frauds
SMOTEInterpolate synthetic frauds between neighboursImplausible records on mixed-type data
EasyEnsembleModels on balanced subsamples, combinedStrong, but costlier to train
Class weightsRaise the loss for missed fraud (scale_pos_weight)Distorts probabilities: recalibrate
Focal lossDown-weight easy negatives (γ ≈ 2)Needs a custom objective

Four SMOTE pitfalls

  1. Before the split, it copies test frauds into training. Resample inside each training fold only.
  2. Interpolating one-hot merchants gives “0.4 of one merchant”. Use SMOTE-NC.
  3. The midpoint between card testing and bust-out resembles neither.
  4. It changes the base rate, so scores stop being probabilities.

Recommended order

  1. Unweighted model + threshold tuning
  2. Then class weights
  3. Then anything more elaborate

On tabular fraud data, class weighting in a boosted tree usually matches or beats SMOTE at a fraction of the complexity.

Calibration: is a score of 0.10 really a 10% chance?

A model can rank well while being badly calibrated. Expected-loss sums, banded thresholds and consortium score-sharing all treat the output as a probability.

Fix with Platt scaling or isotonic regression on a later, unresampled sample. Calibration drift is often the first visible symptom of population drift.

Module 5 · The models

The interpretable baselines

Logistic regression

p̂ = σ(wᵀx + b) = 1 / (1 + e^−(wᵀx+b))

Linear in the log-odds: each coefficient is the change in log-odds per unit of its feature. Calibrated, fast, auditable.

Weakness: sees only the interactions you engineer. Role: the regulatory baseline. If a complex model can't beat a well-specified logistic regression on AUC-PR, its complexity isn't earning its keep.

Decision tree

Gini(S) = 1 − Σ pₖ²   Gain = I(parent) − Σ (nᶜ/n) I(child)

Recursive yes/no splits chosen to purify the children. Captures interactions automatically, needs no scaling, and the rules can be read aloud.

Weakness: high variance. A single tree is a poor fraud model but an excellent explanatory device.

Module 5 · The models

Bagging reduces variance. Boosting reduces bias.

Random Forest (bagging)

f̂(x) = (1/B) Σ T_b(x)   Var ≈ ρσ² + (1−ρ)σ²/B

Many deep trees on bootstrap samples, each split considering a random subset of features, averaged. Hard to overfit, tolerant of noisy labels. A strong low-maintenance baseline.

Gradient boosting

F_m(x) = F_{m−1}(x) + ν · h_m(x)

Shallow trees built in sequence, each fitted to the errors (gradients) of the ensemble so far. Composes many small splits into exactly the non-linear, interaction-heavy boundary fraud needs.

Module 5 · The models

Deep learning: earn the cost, or don't pay it

z = Σ wⱼxⱼ + b,   a = g(z)   ·   W ← W − η ∂L/∂W

A neuron is a weighted sum followed by a non-linearity; backpropagation applies the chain rule backwards and takes a gradient step. Expressive power is not the constraint in fraud detection; labelled positives are.

Why trees usually win on tables

Tabular features are heterogeneous and often irrelevant. Axis-aligned splits handle this natively; a dense layer must learn to ignore inputs and prefers smooth functions.

The useful hybrid

Learn entity embeddings for merchants and BINs in a small network, then feed them into a boosted tree. Reserve an “unknown” token for new merchants. Use stratified batching: a large batch at 0.2% prevalence may contain no fraud at all.

Document forensics

Font and spacing inconsistency, missing security features, template mismatch. Error-level analysis localises the edited region. Transfer learning from pre-trained backbones is essential because forged-document datasets are tiny.

Face embeddings

L = max(0, ‖f(a)−f(p)‖² − ‖f(a)−f(n)‖² + α)

1:1 verification (selfie vs document) and 1:N identification (is this face already on file under another name?). Error rates differ across demographic groups: test disaggregated.

Sequences, not rows

LSTM and GRU gates preserve gradients over long sequences. Uses: account takeover from login-navigation sequences, bust-out building over weeks, card-testing bursts.

The pragmatic verdict

Well-built velocity features often capture most of what a sequence model learns, at a fraction of the operational cost. Sequence models earn their place when the order carries the signal.

Attention

Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax(QKᵀ/√dₖ)V

Every position relates to every other in one step, so a payment can be linked to a specific earlier login or first transfer to the same beneficiary.

Where evidence stands

TabNet, FT-Transformer and SAINT compete on large tables, but boosted trees remain at least competitive at the sizes most institutions hold. Deep models earn their place when sequence, text or images must be fused with the table.

How it works

x → encoder → z (k ≪ d) → decoder → x̂ ; score = ‖x − x̂‖²

Train on genuine transactions only. Unfamiliar patterns reconstruct poorly. Set the threshold from review capacity, not an arbitrary sigma.

Hazard

If fraud contaminates the “normal” training set, the model learns to reconstruct it. High error means unusual, not criminal.

  • Sending customer data to an external model is a disclosure
  • Linguistic “deception markers” are weak: guide attention, never a finding
Use language models to compress an analyst's reading time. Do not use them to reach the conclusion.

Module 6 · Systems in production

Anti-money-laundering is a different problem

Batch, not real-time

Scenario rules defined with the regulator in mind, run over days and weeks of activity.

The incumbent problem

Rule scenarios produce very large alert volumes with low confirmation rates, consuming investigator capacity on cases that were never suspicious.

Explanation required

Every suspicious transaction report must state a rationale an investigator can adopt and defend.

The sequence supervised institutions actually follow

1

Prioritise existing alerts

Rank rule alerts by likelihood of confirmation. Nothing is suppressed, so no obligation is weakened.

2

Tune scenario thresholds with evidence

Use outcome data to show which scenarios and thresholds actually find laundering.

3

Add network detection alongside

Graph methods for mule chains, layering and beneficial-ownership links that scenario rules cannot express.

For financial intelligence units

  • Rank incoming STRs by likelihood of substantiation
  • Link separate reports that concern the same accounts or devices
  • Spot institutions reporting far less than comparable peers

Why not replace rules?

Scenario coverage is examinable. A model that silently suppresses alerts creates supervisory exposure. Ranking is adopted before replacement.

Module 6 · Systems in production

Models decay. Measure how fast.

PSI = Σ_b (p_b − q_b) · ln(p_b / q_b)
Try itPopulation Stability Index for “transaction amount”

Data drift

Inputs shift: new channel, new merchants, season. Detect with PSI or KS per feature. PSI above about 0.2 warrants investigation.

Concept drift

The relationship changes because fraudsters adapt. Detect by decay in precision and recall on matured labels, and by calibration drift before that.

Label delay

Outcomes arrive weeks later. Watch leading indicators: score distribution, alert volume, feature drift.

Champion–challenger: run the new model in shadow against live traffic, compare on matured labels, and promote only on evidence. Never promote on offline metrics alone. Scheduled retraining plus drift-triggered retraining, with the same validation discipline as the first build.

Module 7 · Case studies

Five institutions, five architectures

Drawn from publicly described approaches. Each solves a different problem, and each teaches one transferable lesson.

Problem

Customers legitimately pay from many devices, countries and currencies, so fixed rules raise constant false alarms.

Approach

  • Deep networks on very large volumes, modelling each user's behaviour
  • Geolocation, device fingerprints and behaviour fused with transaction data
  • Transfer learning across regions with sparse fraud
  • A separate anomaly path for login patterns (account takeover)

Lesson

The unit of analysis is the user, not the transaction. Login-time and payment-time detection are separate problems with separate models.

Problem

A network between issuers and merchants must answer within the milliseconds a terminal waits.

Approach

  • Ensembles of tree models and neural networks
  • Many data points per transaction across account, merchant, device and network
  • Behavioural biometrics as an extra signal
  • A probability plus reason codes, so the issuing bank keeps the decision

Lesson

A bare score cannot be actioned, contested or audited. Insist on reasons alongside every score. A network also sees cross-issuer patterns no single bank can.

Problem

Once crypto assets move on-chain, recovery is effectively impossible, so onboarding is the control point.

Approach: identity pipeline

  1. ID capture and quality checks: glare, blur, screen photos, live capture
  2. Document verification: template, fonts, hologram, MRZ, tampering
  3. Face matching with liveness (1:1)
  4. Account-link analysis: repeated faces, shared devices and details
  5. ML risk score plus human review of flagged cases
  6. Decision: approve, resubmit, or escalate

Lessons

Where funds cannot be recovered, move detection to the door. Segment users (active traders vs long-term holders) so “normal” is defined per segment.

Company-described controls; not a claim that every check is automated.

Approach

  • NLP over internal emails, messages and meeting transcripts

What it produces

Not a finding of wrongdoing: a prioritised queue for trained human reviewers.

Governance first

Defined scope, retention limits, access controls, staff notice and a documented review process before switching on.

Federated learning across banks

Each bank trains the model on its own data; only model updates, not customer records, are shared and combined. The joint model learns patterns, such as mule accounts spread across institutions, that no single bank's data reveals.

Why it matters

The strongest signal about a receiving account often sits at a different bank. Federated approaches aim to capture that without pooling personal data, though governance, trust and update-leakage risks still need design.

Problem

Huge volumes of small instant transfers across UPI, NEFT and IMPS, settled irreversibly, concentrated on mobile.

Approach

  • Models built on Indian payment behaviour, not transplanted card models
  • Device intelligence and app-usage signals where the volume sits
  • CNNs for identity documents at onboarding
  • Chatbots triaging customer queries on suspicious transactions

Lessons

A model trained elsewhere reads a festival-season surge of small UPI transfers as coordinated fraud: localisation is a requirement. On instant rails detection must be inline, and at UPI scale precision@k governs.

Module 7 · Case studies

What every case had in common

1 · Each record was individually unremarkable

No single payment, invoice or award would have failed an ordinary check.

2 · The signal came from comparison

Against the person's own history, against other records, or against the network.

3 · The data was usually already held

In most cases nothing external was needed; the records were simply never examined together.

4 · Score and decision were separate

The model emitted a probability; thresholds and actions sat in a decision layer the business controls.

5 · The output was a case, not a verdict

A prioritised lead with reasons. An officer established what had happened.

6 · Timing decided the outcome, and the loop kept it current

Detection before funds moved prevented loss. Recorded outcomes became tomorrow's training labels.

None of these is an algorithm. They are architectural and organisational choices, and they decide outcomes more than model selection does.

Module 8 · Risk, governance & implementation

A fraud decision restricts access to someone's own money

Historical bias

Labels record where fraud was looked for, not only where it occurred. The model learns the enforcement pattern alongside the crime.

Proxy variables

Removing a protected attribute does not remove it: postcode, device and channel can encode it. Report precision and recall by segment.

Over-trust

Once a score is on screen, people stop questioning it. The score becomes the conclusion instead of the starting point.

Guilt by association

Neighbourhood-risk features attribute risk because of who someone transacted with. Use them to prioritise review, never to decide.

A documented precedent: Amazon abandoned an experimental AI recruiting tool after finding it penalised women's applications. The bias came from the historical data, not the algorithm.

Six questions for any vendor, and where to start

AskWhy
“How many alerts per day, and how many are confirmed?”Tests fit with review capacity, which accuracy never reveals
“How much fraud did it miss in testing?”The least reported figure, and your actual loss
“Was it tested on data from a market like ours?”A system tuned elsewhere flags normal local behaviour
“Can it tell an officer why it flagged a case?”Without reasons you cannot answer a complaint
“What happens when it is wrong about a citizen?”There must be a route to challenge and reverse
“Who retrains it, how often, and on whose data?”Decides whether it stays useful, and who controls your data

Implementation stages

1 · Organise the dataConsistent, joinable, retained records. Improves manual investigation immediately.
2 · Record every outcomeA system can only learn from outcomes that were written down. Cheapest, highest value.
3 · One narrow useDuplicate invoices, alert ranking: data exists and results are easy to verify.
4 · Scale with evidenceReal-time scoring, networks, identity. Only after 1–3 work.

The most common, most expensive mistake

Buying stage-4 tools before stages 1 and 2 exist, and spending the budget on licences rather than data and people.

Cooperation, available now at no cost

Agree a minimum structured referral: account IDs, timestamps, device information, amounts. Structured requests between banks, agencies and countries are answered far faster than narrative ones.

Wrap-up

Seven things to carry out of the room

1 · Accuracy is the wrong question

For rare events read precision, recall and the confusion matrix, in counts.

2 · Data and features beat algorithms

Honest labels and chronological validation matter more than model family.

3 · Leakage is the default failure

An excellent fraud result is leakage until an audit proves otherwise.

4 · Trees still win on tables

Deep learning earns its place where sequence, text, image or graph data must be fused.

5 · The threshold is a business decision

Who sets it, on what cost basis, and with what review capacity.

6 · Explainability is a requirement

A decision restricting money must be justifiable to customer, auditor and regulator.

7 · The adversary adapts

Models drift, attacks target the model, and generative tools make fraud cheaper.

The model narrows the search. The judgement stays human.
